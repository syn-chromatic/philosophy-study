## **Thales:**

**First Greek Philosopher (\~624-546 BC)**



Lived in Miletus, a coastal city on the western coast of Anatolia (present-day Turkey), in the region of Ionia. Ionia was home to Greek settlements and served as an important shipping route connecting the Greek world with Anatolia.



Thales was the first of many things, he was the first philosopher, western astronomer, scientist, and Greek mathematician.



He does not have any record of his books, but he was first to predict a solar eclipse, and this can be traced down to an exact date of May 28th, 585 BC.



His main contribution to Philosophy was his search for the Arche that asked what is the universe made of? the stuff that connects everything, the stuff the universe is made of.



The arche had to explain these things:

* It had to explain existence, and the ground for existence itself.
* It had to explain change, how things grow and shrink, and what is the cause of that.
* It had to explain motion, how things change from one place to another
* It had to explain life



His conclusion for what everything is made of is Water, because it explains existence as the substance exists in three states, as a solid, liquid, or gas, and it explains change, because it changes its own form to other states, as it can melt, freeze, condense, and evaporate on any surface, all by itself, and it explains motion, because it also moves all by itself, as he observed the ocean and how it moves all on its own, no one is pushing the waves, and it explains life because all living things depend on it to survive, because without water life does not exist.





## **Anaximander:**

**Second Greek Philosopher (\~610-546 BC)**



Was born probably a few years after Thales, but died at about the same time, like Thales he lived in Miletus and was seen as Thales's student.



Anaximander took science into a whole new level, it is said he went to Sparta to set up a Gnomon, this is a simple pillar that is set up straight with markings on the ground, and when the sun shines you can where and how long the shadow is, that way you can do measurements for time and calendar dates.



He was the first to attempt a map of the entire world.



His theories:

* He believed that the earth did not float on water like Thales did
* He believed that the earth is floating in empty space, because he did not believe that there is absolute up or down
* He disagreed with Thales that the world was a flat disk
* He believed that the earth was a cylinder with 3:1 ratio of its dimensions
* He believed that the sun and the stars were not objects but were rings of fire covered in smoke, and that the sun was just part of the ring peeking from the ring of fire



He is seen as the "Father of Evolution" because of his theory of evolution, since he believed that all life must have changed over time, and that the first type of animals must have been fish or fish-like and water-based, and that humans must have descended from some kind of animal.



For example his reasoning for speculating about the animal origin of humans is because humans nurture their children for several years while most animal young are independent much quicker, so he believed that humans used to live and grow inside animals until puberty because there's no other way they could have survived.



And for the origin of the first animals, they were formed in the sea or some kind of warm water, but they weren't living at normal and free life, instead they lived inside a cocoon-like structure, that was made out of thorny bark, and they kept living inside those cocoons until they migrated to land and broke free.



Anaximander added another theory for Thales's theory of life, by believing that everything had opposites, for example the opposites of heat and cold, or the opposites of wet and dry, and if they combine they cancel each other out.



In modern day, we would refer to this as exchange of energy, but in Anaximander's time, he considers it as an obligatory penalty, rather than a voluntary one, as opposites must pay their penalty eventually, he even took it one step further by saying that the hot summer had to pay a fine to the moderate temperature, because of an injustice that was committed against it, and similarly with cold winter.



He believed that opposites have separated out from the original Arche/Substance, and that all opposites are infinite because they do not have a material substance, so they are not finite in resource, and he called this infinite substance Apeiron, and he referred to it as The Divine.

#### **Summary of all three Greek Philosophers (The Milesians):**

* ###### **Thales:** Formulated the primary question of cosmology by searching for a single physical material (water) to account for the physical state, motion, and origin of all observable phenomena.
* ###### **Anaximander:** Critiqued Thales' reliance on a specific material element, arguing that a single finite substance could not yield its opposite (e.g., water generating fire). He proposed the abstract, boundless Apeiron, introducing principles of cosmic equilibrium and biological transmutation.
* ###### **Anaximenes:** Synthesized both positions by retaining Anaximander's boundless, dynamic qualities while grounding the arche in a observable substance (air). By introducing rarefaction and condensation, he offered the first formal mechanism explaining how quantitative changes in density yield qualitative material transformations.

## **Pythagoras:**

**A New Philosopher (\~570-490 BC)**



Pythagoras was born in Samos, a Greek island off the coast of Anatolia (Turkey), some have speculated that in his youth, he has studied in Miletus, as there was some overlap between him and the Milesians, or that he went to Egypt to study.



Pythagoras has fleed to Croton, a town in the south of Italy which at the time was controlled by Greece, he moved there as dictators began governing cities as well as his island.



Pythagoras founded a school in Croton and started teaching philosophy, math, music, and astronomy.

Which was also considered a cult, as his teachings were said to have been received from divine revelation, and started brainwashing his followers and set a complex set of arbitrary rules that were all shrouded in secrecy.



The rules were:

* They were not allowed to stir fire with a knife
* They had to rub out marks found in pots in ashes or mattresses
* They were not allowed to wear rings
* Whenever they clipped their nails or trimmed their hair, they had to spit on the trimmings
* They were not allowed to have swallows inside their houses
* They were not allowed to urinate facing the sun
* They were not allowed to sacrifice white roosters





Pythagoras believed in transmigration of the soul, in the sense that after you die, your soul migrates to another body of any animal, human included.



He had an obsession for numbers and put meaning to it, where number 2 was female, number 3 was male, and if you combine them it makes number 5 meaning for marriage, and 4 for justice.



He also put meanings to numbers in form of geometry, where number 1 was a dot, 2 a line, 3 a surface, 4 a solid object.



Pythagoras was enamored with the idea of opposites, and he compiled a list of 10 fundamental opposites of reality:

1  Unlimited  |  Limit

2  Even       |  Odd

3  Plurality  |  One

4  Left       |  Right

5  Female     |  Male

6  Motion     |  Rest

7  Bent       |  Straight

8  Dark       |  Light

9  Evil       |  Good

10 Oblong     |  Square







## **Xenophanes:**

**A New Philosopher-Theologian (\~570-475 BC)**



He was originally from Colophon, an Ionian city not far from Miletus and Samos, but at the age of about 25, he started moving to several different places all throughout the Greek-speaking world, going at least as far as Syracuse in Sicily (an island next to south of Italy).



He was also one of the earliest philosophers who left us some of his writing, but he did not write engaging prose dialogues like Plato, nor technical logical arguments like Aristotle, his writing style was more in-line with what Homer or Hesiod did, because he wrote in a poetic style.



He was not a fan of people glorifying athletes like sprinters or boxers, because they received high honors without providing any practical benefit to the city.



He criticized popular Greek religion and myths (like Homer and Hesiod) for assigning human moral flaws to the gods, such as theft, adultery, and deception.



He criticized Anthropomorphism:

* Cultures project their own traits onto gods (e.g., dark/snub-nosed for Ethiopians, red-haired/blue-eyed for Thracians).
* If horses or oxen could draw, they would depict gods looking like horses or oxen.



His Epistemology:

* Knowledge isn't revealed by gods at birth; humans uncover better truth over time through inquiry.
* First to distinguish absolute knowledge from true belief (certainty vs. justified belief without direct proof).



His Science:

* Earth is infinitely wide and deep, extending downward forever (not floating).
* Sun/stars are fiery clouds formed by earth vapors/sparks.
* First to note marine fossils on mountains, deducing that land was once underwater.



His Arche:

* Combination of Water and Earth (wet and dry).



His Theology:

* Believed in one non-anthropomorphic God: infinite, all-seeing, all-hearing, and all-thinking.
* God is motionless, unchanging, and governs everything by mind alone.
* Rejected born or dying gods: claiming a god is born means there was a time God did not exist.
* Introduced the metaphysical concept of Being vs. Not-Being (existence vs. non-existence).





## **Parmenides:**

**A New Philosopher of Being (\~515-445 BC)**



Was born in Elea, a Greek colony in the south of Italy, not far from Croton where Pythagoras had set up his school. Some say he was connected with the Pythagoreans in his youth, and he might have also been influenced by Xenophanes, since Xenophanes had also lived in southern Italy, and Parmenides's idea of one unchanging reality sounds a lot like Xenophanes's one unchanging God.



He wrote only one work, a poem called On Nature, written in the same poetic style as Homer, Hesiod and Xenophanes. In the poem, he is taken on a chariot ride to the home of a goddess, and she reveals the truth to him, so just like Pythagoras, his teaching is presented as a divine revelation, but unlike Pythagoras, the goddess doesn't give him a list of rules, she gives him an argument.



The poem is divided in two parts:

* The Way of Truth (Aletheia), what reason tells us about reality
* The Way of Opinion (Doxa), the deceptive world that our senses show us, which mortals wrongly trust



His main contribution to philosophy was shifting the question. The Milesians asked what is the world made of, and Parmenides asked what does it even mean for something to be? He was the first to build a philosophy out of pure logical reasoning, rather than observation.



The goddess says there are only three possible paths of inquiry:

* It is (what exists, exists)
* It is not (what does not exist)
* It both is and is not (the path that mortals wander on)



Path 2 is impossible, because you cannot know or speak of what is not, as "nothing" is not anything, so if you think or say something, it must be something that is. Path 3 is impossible too, as it contradicts itself, so the only path left is that what is, is.



From this one starting point, he deduced all of the characteristics of Being:

* It cannot come into being, because it would have to come from nothing, and from nothing, nothing comes. It would also have to come from something that is, which means it already existed.
* It cannot perish, for the same reason, it can't turn into nothing.
* It is one and whole, there is nothing that could divide it, because the thing that divides it would have to be "not-being", which doesn't exist.
* It is unchanging, because change means that something is what it was not before, which means not-being turned into being.
* It is motionless, because to move you need an empty space to move into, and empty space is nothing, and nothing does not exist, so there is no void.
* It is complete and finite, he described it like a perfect sphere, evenly balanced from the center in every direction.



His conclusions about the Milesians:

Change, motion, plurality and the arche turning into other things are all illusions, because they require not-being to exist. If Anaximenes's air condenses into water, then air has "stopped being" and water has "come to be", which according to Parmenides is impossible.



The senses can't be trusted, because they show us a world of change and many things, while reason shows us the opposite. When reason and the senses disagree, you follow reason. This is called Rationalism, and it separates him from the Milesians, who relied on observing nature.



Parmenides's argument is the first example of deductive reasoning in philosophy: start with a premise, and follow it logically to a conclusion, even if the conclusion sounds absurd, you can't reject it unless you find a flaw in the premises.



In the Way of Opinion, he still explains the world of appearances using the opposites, Light and Night (fire and earth), similar to the Pythagorean list, but he says this is only a "likely story" and not the truth.



Legacy:

* His student Zeno of Elea defended his teacher's claims by creating paradoxes that showed motion leads to contradictions (like Achilles and the tortoise).
* Plato wrote a dialogue called Parmenides, where an older Parmenides meets a young Socrates in Athens (\~450 BC).
* Later philosophers (Empedocles, Anaxagoras and the Atomists) tried to save change and motion without breaking his logic, by saying that Being isn't one thing but many unchanging things (like atoms), that just rearrange.





Parmenides Claims:

* Whatever is, is
* Things cannot come into existence
* Things cannot be destroyed
* No change
* No motion
* No time







## **Heraclitus:**

**A New Philosopher of Change (\~535-475 BC)**



He lived in Ephesus, it was a center for Greek culture, north of Miletus, he has only written one book that was lost and only quotes of it have survived from other authors who have quoted his book.



Because of this, his quotations are often seen as seemingly contradictory and doesn't form a complete picture because of his complex arguments and thoughts, so it is possible to come into many different conclusions.





Fragment 2:

&#x09;"It is wise not to listen to me,

&#x09;but to the word (Logos),

&#x09;and to confess

&#x09;that all things are one."



In this fragment, when he says "all things are one" it seems like a clear indication that he agreed with the underlying premise of Thales big question "what is the one substance that everything is made of" 



But in the first half of this fragment he said to "listen to the logos (word)", but it can be understood as reason or logic, if that is the case, then Heraclitus invites his listeners not to blindly accept his word for it, but to think for themselves.





At other points he also engages with other parts of earlier philosophers such as:

* Anaximander's Apeiron (source of all things, Arche split in opposites)
* Pythagoras and Xenophanes (both emphasized the importance of opposites)



Heraclitus on the other hand seems to oppose the existence of opposites altogether and rejects that idea outright.





Fragment 16:

&#x09;"The learning of many things does not teach understanding,

&#x09;else would it have taught Hesiod and Pythagoras,

&#x09;and again Xenophanes and Hecataeus."





He denied the existence of opposites in the following quotes:

Fragment 69:

&#x09;"The way up and the way down is one and the same"

Fragment 70:

&#x09;"In the circumference of a circle the beginning and end are common"

Fragment 50:

&#x09;"The straight and the crooked path of the fuller's comb is one and the same"







The famous river quote:

"You cannot step into the same river twice, because the second time you step into the river, both you and the river have changed"



Fragments 41 \& 42:

&#x09;"You cannot step twice into the same rivers;

&#x09;for fresh waters are ever flowing in upon you"



Fragment 81:

&#x09;"We step and do not step into the same rivers;

&#x09;we are and are not"





Which is in opposition to Parmenides that said change was impossible, as he said that you cannot step into a river once, because there no change or motion.



Unlike the Milesians who picked Water, Apeiron and Air to describe the reality of the Arche, Heraclitus used Fire to connect everything.



According to Fragment 22:

&#x09;"All things are an exchange for fire,

&#x09;and fire for all things,

&#x09;even as wares for gold

&#x09;and gold for wares."



But at another point when he seems to be talking about the nature of reality, he says this:

Fragment 83:

&#x09;"It rests by changing"



Fragment 84:

&#x09;"Even the posset (barley drink) separates if it is not stirred"



Parmenides \& Zeno:

Things cannot change (Whatever is, is)

## **Empedocles:**

**A New Philosopher of the Four Roots (\~484-424 BC)**



He lived in Akragas (modern Agrigento), a wealthy Greek city on the island of Sicily, which is close to southern Italy where Parmenides and the Pythagoreans were, so he was probably influenced by both of them, and he is often seen as a student or follower of Parmenides.



He wrote two poems, On Nature and Purifications, both written in verse like Xenophanes and Parmenides, and a lot more of his work has survived compared to most Presocratics, around 450 lines of fragments.



He was also a very colorful character, he was a politician who supported democracy in his city, a doctor and a wonder-worker, and he claimed that he was a god walking among mortals. According to legend he died by jumping into the volcano Mount Etna to prove that he was divine, though this is most likely just a story made up later.



He came after both Heraclitus and Parmenides, so he was facing a problem: Parmenides said that change is impossible, while Heraclitus said that everything is always changing, and Empedocles tried to find a way to fit both of them together.



According to his naturalistic explanation of the universe is the four elements, which are:

* Fire
* Earth
* Air
* Water 



he called them the four "roots" (rhizomata), and they were the first time that a philosopher combined all of the Milesians' proposals into one system, instead of choosing one of them as the arche. Each of the roots is eternal and unchanging, so it can't come into being or perish, which agrees with Parmenides that Being cannot be created or destroyed, but they can mix with each other and separate in different proportions, just like a painter mixes a few colors to produce countless others.



So change is not one thing turning into another like Anaximenes believed, it is only the mixing and separation of the same four roots, what we call birth is mixture and what we call death is separation. This way Parmenides was right that Being cannot change, and Heraclitus was right that the world is always changing.
